# Safe manual lifting and the NIOSH lifting equation

## Why lifting technique and task design matter
Manual lifting is one of the most common causes of back injury in warehouses, loading bays and production lines. The risk depends less on the weight alone than on where the load starts and ends: a light box picked up from the floor at arm's length while twisting can be more harmful than a heavier box lifted close to the body at waist height. For that reason good practice concentrates on the layout of the task first and on individual technique second.

## The idea behind the NIOSH equation
The revised NIOSH lifting equation, published by the US National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health in 1991, calculates a Recommended Weight Limit (RWL) for a specific two-handed lift. It starts from a load constant of 23 kg, the weight that nearly all healthy workers can lift under ideal conditions, and multiplies it by six factors between 0 and 1 that describe how far the real lift is from ideal: RWL = 23 kg x HM x VM x DM x AM x FM x CM. Each factor shrinks the limit when the lift becomes harder.

## The six multipliers in plain words
The horizontal multiplier (HM) falls as the hands move away from the body; it is 1 when the load is within 25 cm and 25/H for a horizontal distance of H centimetres. The vertical multiplier (VM) is best when the hands start at about knuckle height (75 cm) and falls for lifts from the floor or above the shoulders (VM = 1 - 0.003 x |V - 75|). The distance multiplier (DM) penalises long vertical travel. The asymmetric multiplier (AM) falls by about 0.32 percent for every degree the body has to twist (AM = 1 - 0.0032 x A). The frequency multiplier (FM) falls as lifts per minute and the length of the work period increase. The coupling multiplier (CM) is lower when the box has no good handles or hand holds.

## The lifting index
The Lifting Index (LI) is the actual load divided by the RWL. An index of 1 or less means the lift is acceptable for nearly all workers. Above 1 the risk of low-back injury increases for part of the workforce, and an index above 3 is generally treated as high risk where the task should be redesigned. Calculating which multiplier is smallest shows directly what to fix first: a low vertical multiplier points to the floor-level start height, a low horizontal multiplier to reaching, and a low asymmetric multiplier to twisting.

## Practical rules for floor-level lifting
Avoid starting lifts from the floor: put pallets on stands or scissor-lift tables so the load starts between knee and waist height, and use spring-loaded pallet levellers that rise as the stack gets lower. Keep the load close to the body and the path in front of the worker so that the feet, not the spine, turn the body. Reduce the weight of each unit or arrange team lifts for heavy items, and give boxes cut-out handles. Where loads stay heavy or frequent, use vacuum lifters, hoists or conveyors instead of people.

## Frequency, recovery and early warning signs
Repeated lifting fatigues the back muscles, so even acceptable lifts become risky when they are done hundreds of times a shift without breaks. Short pauses spread across the shift and alternating lifting work with lighter tasks help the tissues recover. Supervisors should treat complaints of back or shoulder discomfort, workers rubbing their lower back, or a change in lifting technique late in the shift as early warning signs and review the task before an injury happens.
