# Job rotation guidance for repetitive and heavy work

## What job rotation is and is not
Job rotation means moving workers between tasks at planned times during a shift so that no one carries the most demanding work all day. It spreads physical exposure across the team and across different body regions. It does not reduce the total amount of risky work that the tasks contain: if a station requires deep bending every minute, somebody still has to do it. Rotation is therefore an administrative control that sits below engineering controls (redesigning the workstation, adding lift aids) and should be used together with them, not instead of them.

## Combine tasks that load different body regions
A rotation scheme only helps if the tasks differ. Moving a worker from one bending-heavy task to another bending-heavy task gives the lower back no recovery. Good schemes alternate heavy and light work and alternate body regions, for example floor-level lifting (lower back), followed by visual inspection at a bench (low load), followed by overhead assembly (shoulders), followed by packing at waist height. Each task should be classified by its main physical demand before the rotation order is decided.

## How often to rotate
Rotation intervals of one to two hours are common in manufacturing. Shorter intervals spread exposure more evenly but every change costs set-up time, quality checks and concentration, so rotating too often creates its own problems. A useful rule is to rotate at natural breaks in the work and to move people off the heaviest station before their cumulative exposure for the day reaches the agreed limit, rather than rotating everyone on a fixed timetable regardless of the actual load.

## Training, fairness and participation
Workers can only rotate into tasks they are trained and qualified for, so cross-training is a precondition for effective rotation; a team in which only one person can do the heaviest job cannot share it. Schemes work best when workers help design them, understand why they rotate and see that the heaviest tasks are shared fairly. Individual differences such as previous injuries, medical restrictions or a temporary reduced capacity must be respected, and the plan should be approved by a supervisor who can take these into account.

## Monitoring exposure and adjusting the plan
A rotation plan should be checked against measured exposure, not only against intentions. Recording posture risk scores per worker and station across the shift shows whether exposure is really being shared, which station drives most of the risk and whether someone is accumulating too much. If one station keeps dominating the exposure of everybody who works there, that station is a priority for engineering improvements. Plans should be reviewed when the product mix, staffing or station layout changes.

## Rest breaks and recovery
Recovery time matters as much as task variety. Short micro-pauses of a few seconds within a task, a brief break every hour or two, and moving to a light task after a heavy one all allow muscles and tendons to recover. Workers who already report discomfort, are new to a physically demanding task or are returning from an injury need a lighter mix and should build up exposure gradually over several days.
