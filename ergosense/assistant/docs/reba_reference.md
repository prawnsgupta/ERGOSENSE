# REBA scoring reference

## What REBA is for
REBA (Rapid Entire Body Assessment) is an observational checklist for judging how risky a working posture is for musculoskeletal disorders such as low-back pain and shoulder strain. It was published by Hignett and McAtamney in 2000 and is meant for tasks where the whole body is involved, for example handling boxes, assembly at varying heights or work with frequent bending. An assessor looks at one posture (usually the worst or the most frequent one), gives each body part a small score, combines them through three lookup tables and ends with one number from 1 to 15. Higher numbers mean the posture needs attention sooner.

## Group A: trunk, neck and legs
The trunk scores 1 when upright, 2 for up to 20 degrees of bending forward or backward, 3 for 20 to 60 degrees of forward bending (or more than 20 degrees leaning back) and 4 beyond 60 degrees of forward bending. One point is added if the trunk is also twisted or bent sideways. The neck scores 1 for up to 20 degrees of forward flexion and 2 for more flexion or any extension, again plus one point for twisting or side bending. The legs score 1 when weight is on both feet and 2 when standing on one leg or on an unstable base; one point is added for knee bending of 30 to 60 degrees and two points for more than 60 degrees. Table A combines the three into a single Group A value.

## Group B: upper arms, lower arms and wrists
The upper arm is scored from its angle to the trunk: 1 for between 20 degrees behind and 20 degrees in front of the body, 2 for 20 to 45 degrees of raising (or more than 20 degrees behind the body), 3 for 45 to 90 degrees and 4 above 90 degrees. Add one point if the arm is held out to the side or rotated, one if the shoulder is raised, and subtract one if the arm is supported or the posture is helped by gravity. The lower arm scores 1 when the elbow is bent between 60 and 100 degrees and 2 otherwise. The wrist scores 1 within 15 degrees of straight and 2 beyond that, plus one if it is bent sideways or twisted. Table B combines these into a Group B value. REBA is normally scored for the side that is worse.

## Load, coupling and activity
Score A is the Table A value plus a load or force score: 0 for loads under 5 kg, 1 for 5 to 10 kg and 2 for more than 10 kg, with one extra point if the force is applied suddenly or with a jerk. Score B is the Table B value plus a coupling score that rates the grip: 0 for a good handle, 1 for an acceptable grip, 2 for a poor grip and 3 when there is no safe way to hold the object. Table C combines Score A and Score B. Finally one point each is added for holding a body part still for more than a minute, for small movements repeated more than four times a minute, and for rapid large changes in posture or an unstable base.

## Action levels
The final score maps to five action levels. A score of 1 is negligible and needs no action. Scores of 2 and 3 are low risk; a change may be needed. Scores of 4 to 7 are medium risk; the task should be investigated and changed soon. Scores of 8 to 10 are high risk; investigate and change the task promptly. Scores of 11 to 15 are very high risk; the task should be changed immediately. The action level is a priority signal for the safety team rather than a diagnosis for an individual worker.

## Using REBA with a camera
Automatic REBA from a single webcam follows the same rules but estimates the joint angles from body landmarks. Angles in the plane of the image are the most reliable, so the camera should see the worker from the side or at about 45 degrees, with the whole body in view. Depth (movement towards or away from the camera) is the weakest estimate, small hand and wrist movements are hard to see, and load, grip quality and shoulder shrugging cannot be seen at all, so they must be entered by a person. Short spikes caused by tracking errors should be smoothed out over about a second before any alert is raised. Camera-based scores are best used to spot trends and compare stations, and a trained assessor should confirm any decision about redesigning a task.

## Overhead and shoulder-height work
Work with the hands at or above shoulder height scores high in Group B because the upper arm is raised beyond 90 degrees, and workers often shrug the shoulder and tilt the head back at the same time. Even with light parts this loads the shoulder tendons, which recover slowly, so holding the arms up for long periods is a known cause of shoulder disorders. The usual remedies are to bring the work down to between elbow and shoulder height with height-adjustable fixtures or work platforms, tilt or rotate the part so it can be reached from below shoulder level, hang heavy tools from balancers, and limit the continuous time any one person spends working overhead.

## What usually lowers a REBA score
The biggest reductions normally come from removing deep trunk bending (raise the work surface or the pallet, use lift tables), keeping hands between hip and shoulder height (adjust conveyor and shelf heights, use platforms), reducing loads or splitting them, providing good handles, and avoiding twisting by placing the start and end of a movement in front of the worker. Changing the task design is more effective than training workers to adopt better postures on their own.
